Catasterism

By Michael Silberman

When a man stops breathing in a Palm Springs hotel room, calling for help means explaining what happened.

Print ISBN 979-8-9978965-1-5

Chapter One · Excerpt

One Loafer

The man on the bathroom floor was wearing one Gucci loafer.

The other one stood beside the toilet, polished black leather with the heel crushed flat from a year of being stepped into without hands. It pointed at the door as though it had let itself in. Six people were shouting and I noticed the shoe first.

The shower was running. To reach the tap I would have to step over the man on the floor. Behind the steam stood a naked man I had never met, who asked, with real hope, whether I happened to be a doctor.

“No.”

“Do you know what he took?”

“I know some of it.”

“What does that—what does that mean?”

“It means move.”

He moved about a foot.

His name turned out to be Trevor, which I learned by hearing it screamed from four directions by people who had no intention of doing the thing themselves. Trevor, get a towel. Trevor, call downstairs. Trevor, where’s his phone. Trevor, for God’s sake put some pants on.

Trevor stayed at the sink with both hands pressed flat to his cheeks. By then I was working around him like a piece of furniture that happened to be crying.

I had spent years training myself to look first at whatever could not put me in prison. Nothing in the Penal Code mentions a loafer. It still bothers me that I found that part of the room so quickly.

The man on the floor was Julian, thirty-eight, a seller of commercial real estate. For most of a year he had been about to make partner. His company’s website still listed him as an associate when I checked it one day in a parking lot. He had a closet full of Saint Laurent and owed me fourteen hundred dollars.

“Julian.” When he did not answer I slapped the tile beside his ear, hard, twice. “Julian.” His eyelids came up a quarter of the way and went back down.

Three small silver stars were stuck to his forehead. Somebody had been decorating people earlier, in a mood that had since ended, and the party had outlasted the mood long enough that the glitter had quietly turned into medical information.

A woman in a white dress crouched beside me with her fingertips on the tile for balance. She had mascara under one eye and none under the other. “Is he dying?”

“I don’t know.”

Somebody behind me said, “You sell this stuff.”

Nobody answered it, and nobody turned to see who had said it.

“What do we do?” the woman in white said.

“Call 911.”
