Catasterism

A Novel

By Michael Silberman

Print ISBN: 979-8-9978965-1-5
“catasterism (n.): the placing of a person among the stars; a transformation into a constellation.”

A man overdoses on a hotel bathroom floor, six people are shouting, and the one guest paying attention is looking at a shoe: a Gucci loafer standing upright beside the toilet, the only thing in the room that cannot send him to prison. He has spent ten years training his eyes to find things like that.

He was a lawyer once, for about ten months. Since then he has supplied the valley: a surgeon, a songwriter, a Hollywood director, a prep cook who counts his money twice. He has rules. Nobody from the route inside his house. Nobody looks back at him. On Sundays he brings ice to his mother's.

Then a failing art gallery decides it is a church and asks him to write its rules. He cannot lie down at night without drowning, his two dogs are keeping their own record, and the person he loves is still waiting for one true sentence.

Excerpt: Chapter One

The man on the bathroom floor was wearing one Gucci loafer.

The other one stood upright beside the toilet, polished black leather with the heel crushed flat from a year of being stepped into without hands, pointed at the door as though it had let itself in and was waiting to be told where to go next.

I noticed the shoe first.

Six people were shouting at once. Somebody had left the shower running and nobody had thought to turn it off, so the room was filling with steam behind a naked man I had never met in my life, who kept asking me, with real hope, whether I happened to be a doctor.

“No.”
“You—you know what he took?”
“I know some of it.”
“What does that—what does that mean?”
“It means move.”

He moved. His name turned out to be Trevor, which I learned the way you learn anything at a party like that one: by hearing it screamed at thirty-second intervals from four directions by people who had no intention of doing the thing themselves. Trevor, get a towel. Trevor, call downstairs. Trevor, where's his phone. Trevor, for God's sake put some pants on.

Noticing the shoe first bothered me later, and it still bothers me, because I have had a long time to work out what kind of man takes inventory of the footwear in a room where somebody has stopped breathing, and every answer I have come up with is a version of the same answer: I had spent years training myself to look at the one part of a scene that could not put me in prison.
