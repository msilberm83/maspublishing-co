Good for It

Four people owe Eli Mercer money. He has five weeks to make good on a debt of his own.

At an aging Southern California hotel, a canceled wedding and a retirement dinner give Eli a chance to put the people who owe him to work. His sister has questions about the arrangements he’s making through her catering business. So do the people he’s trying to help.

Chapter One

I had come to Dennis's office prepared to be unpleasant about the money he owed me. Then I saw the widow beside his desk and stood in the doorway, looking patient.

She sat like a nun out of uniform, her purse squarely on her lap, watching herself sing to her husband on Dennis's screen. He wore a purple crown and had an elbow on each of two tables pushed together at different heights.

“Purple was his favorite color,” she said.

He made it to the chorus before he glanced at the camera and switched off both hearing aids.

Dennis paused the film and left him caught at it. I looked at the widow, waiting for her to tell him to take that part out.

“Can you make it go a little further? That's where Robbie starts laughing.”

Dennis let it run. The boy slipped sideways out of his chair and caught the tablecloth, pulling a plate against somebody's elbow. His grandfather lowered his hands, the camera swung up, and the woman beside Dennis laughed before we heard the plate break.

Yesterday I had told Dennis I couldn't spend another afternoon hearing why he didn't have my money. When I came in, he had raised a finger without stopping the film. There was an empty chair beside the widow, but I stayed in the doorway.

Dennis moved the film back to her husband's face, then turned toward me. “Ruth, this is Eli. Eli's waiting for me.”

“I'm keeping you.” She shifted her purse without quite turning away from the screen.

“I've got time,” I told her, in front of the man who owed me twenty-one hundred dollars.

“This is my husband, Paul.” Ruth put her hand toward the screen. “That was his birthday.”

I came over and gave her my hand. She held it while looking back at Paul, so I stood beside her until she let go. He still had his fingers beside his ears.

On the other monitor Dennis had two versions open, PAUL FINAL and PAUL FINAL NEW. A strip of little pictures included a swimming pool, a young woman beside a car, and somebody's carpet from several unhelpfully similar angles.

The computer cables ran through a round hole in a cabinet where a dentist's plumbing had been. Pale rectangles marked the walls where the dentist's qualifications had hung.

“The dinner has a wonderful warmth,” he told her. “It's the transition into his parents. I've tried a few different pieces of music.”

He moved the pointer along the pictures, showing her where he would have to make the cut. Ruth listened with her eyes on Paul. When Dennis began to play one of the pieces of music, she put a hand on his arm.

“I just want to see what happens after the plate.”

He returned to the dinner. As the camera came down from the ceiling, Paul took off his crown to help with the broken plate. Ruth put it back on him. He steadied it with one hand and bent to gather the pieces. I glanced at the woman beside me, expecting her to laugh again. She was quiet now, watching him reach under the table. I looked back at the screen.
