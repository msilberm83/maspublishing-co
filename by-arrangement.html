By Arrangement

By the time a woman’s purse is stolen, her boyfriend has spent months rehearsing the rescue.

Martin Sayer arranges apparently spontaneous experiences for paying clients. Then a presidential campaign asks him to stage a public reconciliation with a grieving woman whose loss is tied to his own past.

Chapter One

I was trying to see past a woman carrying a cake when Adrian Lowe smiled at the man stealing his girlfriend's purse. Only briefly, the pleased little flicker of someone who has opened an examination paper and found the question he prepared. He corrected it before Celia turned toward him. From outside the florist, with the purple wrapping of my flowers creaking against my coat, I could still see what his face had done.

Since January he had been preparing for this afternoon, acquiring the ability to run properly, to clear a low obstruction without first arranging his legs, to trust his body for something beyond conveying him between expensive places. He was proud of himself. Reasonably proud, considering what Jonah had started with, although during the final rehearsal he had become so pleased by the ease with which he cleared the wall that he went back to do it again.

Straight into a bucket.

No buckets today. People, though, including this woman who had stopped with her cake exactly where I needed to look. Her arms were extended around its box and her bottom pushed out behind her, as if the cake were driving. I edged sideways, the flowers catching on a brick, and found Lewis already running.

Lewis had a daughter at a school he could not quite afford. He had asked me twice whether Adrian understood that catching him did not include punching him in the face, and twice I had assured him he did. There was always that portion of a performance for which assurances were available and very little else. We hired people who could remember what they were doing while appearing to do something different; our clients sometimes became confused in the middle of getting what they wanted.

Celia cried out. Such a sharp, unprepared sound that I tightened my hand around the flowers, crushing the paper. Adrian was after him now, running, quickly, between two parked vans and emerging across the street where Lewis had reached a delivery cart. For a few seconds their coats gave them the look of two large boys dressed for an interview by the same anxious father. Then Adrian accelerated. Lewis moved economically, holding something in reserve, while Adrian spent everything getting nearer to him.

The effort would have to be his. I had told him that often enough, liking the distinction and the modest limit it appeared to place on what we sold.

A horn sounded. Celia had stepped into the road and came back onto the pavement without looking at the driver, her attention still fixed on Adrian. The cake woman was beside her, saying something I couldn't hear. Behind them, above a boarded shopfront, stone faces peered out from under their eroded eyebrows; somebody had painted two when the shop was renovated, then apparently reconsidered the expense. Newly dead among the weathered ones. In the uncertain April light even the dirt looked freshly settled, as if the street had been washed and somebody had put it all back.
