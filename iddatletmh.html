I Didn't Do Anything to Lose Except Try My Hardest

A Novel of Gambling, Family, and Memory

By Michael Silberman

Print ISBN: 979-8-9978965-0-8
“Downstairs, a quarter on the edge of an arcade cabinet holds your place. Upstairs, nobody comes looking for the adults.”

In 1995, nine-year-old Daniel spends a summer vacation in two desert casino towns with his mother, his brother, and his grandparents. While the grown-ups play the machines, the kids run the arcade on quarters and unwritten law, and a girl named Celia settles disputes at the air-hockey table until the morning she is gone.

Thirty years later, Daniel has lost his accounting career, his house, and, in one night at a high-limit machine, two hundred and ten thousand dollars that were briefly his. A counselor asks for his earliest memory of gambling. To answer, he goes looking for everyone who was on that casino floor: the waitress who knew every guest's drink, the slot technician who knew exactly how the machines lied and played them anyway, the cashier who counted a father's apartment deposit into a white envelope, the host who made a call she still regrets, and the woman who won $611,204.06 four machines away while a nine-year-old clapped.

His grandmother agrees to the camera, reads every page, and corrects every memory that flatters him. She will not let childhood become an alibi. Her question runs under all of it: When did it start.

Excerpt: The Form

The intake office had carpet the color of oatmeal and a clipboard with a pen attached by a chain nobody would ever test.

I had lost everything a person can lose on paper—the money, the credit, the house the money had been pretending to maintain—and I had done it holding two degrees. I went to a good university twice and then got my CPA license, and I say that flatly, because the credentials changed nothing. The disease does not check credentials at the door; it takes the door. I could reconcile a ledger and I could not show up. Anywhere. For anything. A man who cannot show up cannot be employed, housed, or argued with, and by the end I was none of the three, and the street does not validate parking.

The woman behind the desk read my answers upside down while I wrote them, professionally, and did not hurry me. The form wanted the ordinary catalogue. Name. Date of birth. Emergency contact—I gave my mother, and then sat looking at the words emergency and contact for a while, because the form had put them together like it knew us. Education: I wrote college. Then, because the line was long and some part of me still wanted partial credit somewhere, I added my CPA license.

She read it upside down and did not look impressed, which was correct, and I have thought about her professionalism with gratitude ever since. In that office a CPA license was a fact about a man the way a watch is a fact about a wrist. The form did not ask what I had done with my education. The form asked where I had slept last night.

I wrote the truth. It was the first form in years I had filled out without arranging it.

Underneath every page that follows there is one question, and it is not mine. It is the one my grandmother put to me before I had answered anything else, with the camera already running and her coffee going cold on purpose.

When did it start.
