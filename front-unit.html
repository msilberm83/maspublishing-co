The Front Unit

Julian has no idea his neighbor is planning to hurt him. Her husband does.

Nancy records Julian’s visitors and searches his trash. When her complaints fail to turn the neighborhood against him, she begins a campaign that her husband could stop—if he were willing to admit his part in it.

The Front Unit

THE WINDOW OVER MY KITCHEN SINK faces east, and for three hours every morning the glass turns the color of weak tea, and if you don't know to look past that light you will miss half of what happens on this street before nine o'clock. I have never missed it. After eleven years in that chair, I know which unit puts its trash out an hour early, whose marriage has gone quiet in a way that isn't peace, who waves and means it and who waves because the alternative is being asked why she doesn't. It isn't a gift. It's closer to a discipline, the unglamorous kind that nobody applauds and everybody, eventually, needs — and if that sounds like a grievance, it isn't one. I gave up wanting to be thanked for it around the same year I gave up expecting my husband to stand at that window with me.

He never has. Not once in fourteen years, not even the morning a moving truck idled at the curb across the street with its ramp still rattling against the pavement, and a man I had not yet met came around from the driver's side of a car that had plainly cost more than our roof.

I wrote the time down before he'd shut the door behind him. Nine-fourteen. Call that a compulsion if you like; you'd be missing the point. A record is only as good as its exactness, and in eleven years of watching this street come slowly apart and slowly back together, I have found exactness to be the one commodity nobody else here is willing to supply.

The truck was a rental, white, twenty-six feet, and it had backed into place before the front unit the way a thing backs into a space it has no intention of respecting — too fast, too close to the mailbox post, correcting itself at the last second. The unit had stood empty since April. The Ramirezes had lived there five years without incident, which on this street counts as a kind of grace, and then they had gone to be near a daughter in another state, and the hedge along their old walk had gone shaggy in their absence in a way I'd raised at the May meeting and the board had tabled, because the board tables anything that would cost more than the coffee budget.

So it sat there through May and June and July with its blinds down, a missing tooth in an otherwise ordinary smile, and then, on a Saturday morning in August, it stopped being empty.

He came around the car before the movers had the ramp fully down, and he stood on the sidewalk with his hands loose in his pockets, unhurried in a way that struck me even before I understood why it should. Most people arrive at a new place carrying something — nerves, hope, a private bracing for disappointment. This man carried none of it. He looked up at the unit the way a person looks at a thing he has already decided to like, and when one of the movers said something to him, he laughed, easily, and the sound came clean across the street and in through my open window like it had been invited.
