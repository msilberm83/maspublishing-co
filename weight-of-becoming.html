The Weight of Becoming

Daniel ignored his mother’s last call. Eight years later, letters in her handwriting describe things that haven’t happened yet.

When a seven-year-old girl appears in his apartment and calls him Dad, Daniel has to confront a life he never lived. The child needs a place in the life he has now, while his family begins remembering choices none of them made.

The Call Daniel Did Not Answer

On the morning his mother died, Daniel Vale watched the telephone ring until it stopped.

For years afterward, he remembered the light before he remembered the sound. Blue thinned almost to gray, it lay across the unwashed plates and caught the chipped rim of Claire’s white mug, which he held between his hands. A sweater hung from the back of the sofa with both arms empty. Beyond the windows, rooftops and water towers remained indistinct against the approaching daylight, while in the kitchen his mother’s name appeared with an assurance that irritated him. She knew he was awake. He had made the mistake of telling her what time he got up.

He was twenty-six, had known Claire for six months, and considered his independence an established fact which his family unreasonably required him to demonstrate. Margaret’s early calls were a recurring infringement. He had asked her to wait until after work; she had agreed, then continued to telephone whenever she remembered something he ought to know. Her requests generally arrived as information about his father’s fatigue or Ruth’s plans for Sunday. Even the weather could apparently make either visiting or staying away inconsiderate, depending on what his mother had hoped he would do.

He loved her, and could imagine the pleasure she took in arranging a dinner before anyone had declined it. That pleasure was part of the difficulty. An invitation from Margaret reached him already furnished with the meal she meant to cook and the expectation of his father sitting at the other end of the table, entitled to a civility he had done little to earn. Daniel would have to disappoint her before he could explain himself, and by then the explanation would sound unkind. It was easier to let the telephone ring and remain, for another minute, a son who might still come.

His father could apologize first. Daniel had said this to Margaret often enough for her to know precisely where the conversation would end, though she persisted in approaching it from different directions. He was finished with the argument, he insisted; only his family’s refusal to accept this required him to keep having it. At twenty-six he could recognize an unreasonable demand, but had not yet learned to distinguish it from a reasonable need he was free to leave unsatisfied. He might have answered his mother, asked how she was, and declined Sunday dinner. Instead, he stood in the kitchen anticipating the whole of her disappointment until the ringing stopped.

Later, he would remember being in the shower. In another version he had left the telephone beneath unopened mail and found it after the calls had ended. For a time he recalled a single ring, so brief that he could scarcely have crossed the room. These accounts came and went with the circumstances in which he needed them. Several winters after the funeral, a more elaborate memory arrived: he had answered, had said hello into a rush of static, and had waited while his mother tried to speak. He could hear the open line. For almost a week he believed it, comforted by a precision which ought to have made him suspicious, since nothing else about that morning had ever been so accommodating.
